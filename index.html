01 — Positions

Six jobs, one shape

A hockey lineup looks like a fixed set of six, but each job is defined by where it happens: the goal line, the walls, the slot, the blue line. Click a position on the board to read its job in all three zones.

C

Centre

The 200-foot job

The only forward with a built-in responsibility at both nets. Takes the face-off, is the low support on breakouts, and is usually the first forward back as F3.

Own zone
Low slot support, face-off coverage, second man on the puck below the goal line.
Neutral zone
Swing lane through the middle, first pass option, back-pressure on the puck carrier.
Offensive zone
Slot and bumper, net-front screens, low cycle support.
LW

Left Wing

Wall and lane

Works the strong-side wall, which makes him the primary outlet on a rim or a wall battle. Modern wings are also the first forechecker on the puck side.

Own zone
Strong-side wall, point coverage when the D steps up, breakout outlet.
Neutral zone
Wide lane, stretch option, first man in on a dump-in.
Offensive zone
Half-wall on the power play, net-front or slot depending on the system.
RW

Right Wing

Weak-side threat

Mirror of the left wing, but often the designated weak-side shooter: back-door on the power play, far-post on a 2-on-1, and the late man on a cycle.

Own zone
Weak-side wall and back-door coverage, second man high.
Neutral zone
Wide lane with a read: stretch if the puck is won, curl back if it is lost.
Offensive zone
Off-ball movement, weak-side post, one-timer option.
LD

Left Defence

Gap and first pass

Owns the left half of the defensive zone and the break-out start. The modern job is less about hitting and more about the first pass under pressure and stepping into the rush.

Own zone
Net-front box-out, corner retrievals, first pass or rim under forecheck.
Neutral zone
Gap control at the blue line, stand-up or step-up depending on the system.
Offensive zone
Point on the power play, walking the line, shot-pass and activation.
RD

Right Defence

Partner and coverage

Reads off the left defence: if one goes, the other stays. On the power play the right D is often the shooter at the top of the umbrella; on the kill, the net-front blocker.

Own zone
Front-of-net coverage, second retrieve, shot blocking in the lane.
Neutral zone
Support on a pinch, cover the middle if the partner steps up.
Offensive zone
Second point, one-timer threat, keeping the puck alive at the line.
G

Goaltender

Angles and rebound control

The last line, but also the first read: depth and angle decide how much net is actually open. Modern goaltending is a positioning system more than a reflex contest.

Own zone
Square to the puck, cut the angle, control rebounds to the corners, post play.
Neutral zone
Communication, puck play behind the net where the rules allow it.
Offensive zone
Sixth attacker on a delayed penalty, break-out support, setting the puck up.

The modern twist: roles beat numbers

Coaches increasingly describe players by job rather than by listed position — F1 (first forechecker), F2 (support), F3 (high forward), strong-side, weak-side, bumper, net-front. A centre may spend a shift as F1 while a winger covers the middle. The number on the sweater still matters at the face-off dot; after that, the system assigns the job.

02 — Power Play

Five skaters, more space, one timer

A power play is a geometry problem: create a two-on-one somewhere, then move the puck there faster than the penalty kill can rotate. Four structures do most of the work.

1-3-1

One point, three across, one at the net.

    Attacking five Penalty kill four Puck

    Why the 1-3-1 took over

    It spreads the ice, keeps a body in front of the net at all times and puts shooters where they can shoot off the pass. It also rotates into an umbrella or overload look in a single pass, which is why it has largely replaced fixed set plays at the top level — the shape hides the next play. Personnel matters: handedness sets who can one-time from where, and the bumper needs to read a seam rather than stand still.

    03 — Penalty Kill

    Four skaters against five, and still a plan

    The kill chooses what to give up. Every structure is a trade: pressure buys time and risk, a box buys the slot and gives the perimeter. Most modern kills are a blend, and switch between them shift by shift.

    Diamond

    One high, two flanks, one at the net.

      Attacking five Penalty kill four Puck

      The trade every kill makes

      Aggression creates short-handed chances and kills momentum, but one beaten first man turns a 4-on-5 into a 3-on-4 in front of the net. A passive box protects the slot and the back door but concedes the point and the half-walls, which is why so many goals against a box come from a screened shot rather than a seam pass.

      04 — Special Situations

      When the ice is not 5-on-5

      Each board carries a qualitative read on both sides of the risk: how often the structure is expected to convert, and what it gives up going the other way. Most games are decided by the minutes nobody rehearses: a two-man advantage, a pulled goaltender, a delayed call, four-on-four after coincidental minors, three-on-three in overtime. Each situation changes the arithmetic — how many options exist, and which pass becomes available.

        05 — Face-offs & Regroup

        The first three seconds, and the reset

        A face-off is the only moment in hockey where every player starts from a known place — which is why teams rehearse it as a set play. The regroup is its cousin: when the entry is not there, the modern answer is to turn back and come again with five skaters instead of forcing a low-percentage puck.

          06 — Traditional vs Modern

          What actually changed

          The sport did not change its rules so much as its spacing. Traditional systems assigned zones and chased the puck; modern systems assign lanes, stay layered, and treat the middle of the ice as the thing worth defending. Four side-by-side boards show the shift.

          Watch the movement, not the shape: trails show where every skater actually goes, the dashed line is the puck, and the numbered arrows are the passes in order. Traditional boards chase late and leave gaps; modern boards arrive early and slide as a unit.

          A

          Forecheck

          How many skaters chase the puck in the offensive zone, and what the rest protect.

          Traditional · 2-1-2

          Two forwards chase the puck carrier, one sits high, both defencemen hold the line. Pressure comes fast and creates turnovers right away — but it also leaves the middle open behind the first two.

          Modern · 1-3-1 / layered

          One forechecker pressures, three form a wall across the middle, one stays high as a safety. The goal is not the turnover in the corner but the forced pass into the wall.

          Why it shifted: with faster exits and better puck-moving defencemen, an aggressive two-man chase is beaten by one short pass. Layering trades a chance at a turnover for the guarantee that a beaten forecheck does not become an odd-man rush.

          B

          Breakout

          How the puck leaves its own zone under pressure.

          Traditional · rim and chase

          The defenceman fires the puck hard around the boards and everyone races. Simple, low-risk in the moment, and it turns possession into a footrace along the wall.

          Modern · five-man short support

          D-to-D, then a short pass to a centre swinging low, with a winger already moving up the wall as the third option. Every pass is short enough to be a real option under pressure.

          Why it shifted: a rim is a 50-50 puck. A supported breakout keeps possession, and possession is what generates the entries and the offensive-zone time that modern teams measure.

          C

          Neutral zone

          What happens between the two blue lines decides most games.

          Traditional · 1-2-2

          One forechecker, two in the middle, two back. A reactive shape: it forms after the puck is dumped and reacts to where it lands.

          Modern · 1-3-1 trap

          A pre-set wall: one high, three across the centre line, one behind. It invites the pass to the wall and closes it, forcing the dump-in into a set structure.

          Why it shifted: the trap is not passive. It is a shape designed to make the opponent's first pass a bad one, and it converts neutral-zone turnovers into entries with numbers.

          D

          Defensive-zone coverage

          Who is responsible for which attacker in front of the net.

          Traditional · man-on-man

          Every defender locks onto a specific opponent and follows him. Clear accountability, but one lost battle or a switch in the slot opens the prime area immediately.

          Modern · low-zone collapse

          Four collapse into a tight box around the slot, protect the house, and let the point shoot. Coverage becomes area-first, with man responsibilities picked up inside the box.

          Why it shifted: shot-quality analysis made the slot the only area worth over-defending. Collapsing concedes low-danger point shots and removes high-danger tips and back-door plays — then the goaltender is expected to stop what is left.

          08 — Glossary

          The words on the whiteboard

          Commentary borrows coaching language constantly. These are the terms that show up most, in the sense a coach uses them.

          F1 / F2 / F3
          The first, second and third forward in the forecheck or backcheck order. Not fixed to a position — whoever is closest takes F1.
          Strong side
          The side of the ice the puck is on. Most support and most pressure live here.
          Half-wall
          The boards between the goal line and the blue line. The power play's most common puck-handling spot.
          Bumper
          The middle player of a 1-3-1, in the slot between the two half-walls. Finds seams and shoots off the pass.
          Seam
          The passing lane that runs through the middle of a penalty-kill box or diamond. If it opens, someone is out of position.
          Royal road
          The imaginary line through the middle of the offensive zone between the face-off dots. Cross-ice passes across it create the highest-danger chances.
          Gap
          The distance between a defender and the puck carrier. Tight gap means pressure; loose gap means time and speed for the attacker.
          Layer
          Having a second and third skater behind the first, so a beaten check does not become an odd-man rush.
          Activation
          A defenceman joining the rush or stepping below the goal line. The modern four-man attack.
          Cycle
          Keeping the puck alive along the walls and behind the net by rotating support, rather than shooting from distance.
          Back door
          The far post, behind the defence. The target for a cross-ice pass the goaltender cannot reach.
          Box out
          Putting a body between an attacker and the goaltender so a rebound cannot be tipped or buried.
